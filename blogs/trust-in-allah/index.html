Yusuf 12:86

I Complain of My Anguish and Sorrow Only to Allah

A small reflection on grief, sabr, tawakkul, and the kind of hope that still gets up and searches.

August 25, 2026 · Opai Elsheikh

قَالَ إِنَّمَآ أَشْكُوا۟ بَثِّى وَحُزْنِىٓ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ وَأَعْلَمُ مِنَ ٱللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ ٨٦

He replied, “I complain of my anguish and sorrow only to Allah, and I know from Allah what you do not know.”

— Dr. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran. Source: Quran.com, Yusuf 12:86.

The first thing this ayah does is remove the shame from grief. Ya‘qub, peace be upon him, is a prophet. He is not weak in faith. He is not spiritually immature. Yet Surah Yusuf shows him grieving so deeply that his eyes turned white from sorrow. The Qur’an does not hide that. It preserves it.

That matters because a lot of people misunderstand trust in Allah. They think tawakkul means you should never feel shaken. They think sabr means the heart becomes silent, flat, and untouched. But Ya‘qub says something more honest and more beautiful: my pain is real, but I know where to carry it.

The Context

Yusuf 12:86 comes after another blow lands on Ya‘qub’s heart. He has already lost Yusuf, and now his sons return from Egypt without Binyamin. In verse 83, he says he will hold to beautiful patience and hopes that Allah will return them all to him. In verse 84, he turns away and remembers Yusuf. In verse 85, his sons worry that grief is consuming him. Then he answers with this verse: he is not complaining about Allah to people; he is pouring his grief out to Allah.

The next verse is just as important. After saying this, Ya‘qub tells his sons to go and search for Yusuf and his brother, and not to despair of Allah’s mercy. So the Qur’anic pattern is not “feel sad and do nothing.” It is: grieve before Allah, refuse despair, then move.

Trust in Allah is not pretending the wound is small. It is knowing the wound has a Lord.

Complaint to Allah

The wording is intimate. Ya‘qub does not say he has no anguish. He says his anguish and sorrow are directed to Allah. Classical tafsir highlights this distinction: the problem is not having pain, and it is not even speaking pain. The danger is losing adab with Allah, turning pain into accusation, or seeking ultimate rescue from the creation while forgetting the Creator.

Ibn Kathir explains that Ya‘qub complained of the afflictions that struck him to Allah alone, and that he expected goodness from Allah. That line is the whole balance. He had grief, but he also had expectation. He had memory of loss, but also memory of Allah’s promise.

Beautiful Patience

Beautiful patience is not numbness. It is not performance. It is not saying “I’m fine” when you are not. Beautiful patience is when the pain does not make you unjust, hopeless, or disconnected from Allah. Ya‘qub’s grief did not cancel his prophethood, and his tears did not cancel his tawakkul.

That gives the believer permission to be human. You can cry and still trust Allah. You can miss what is gone and still believe Allah knows what you do not know. You can be exhausted and still be on the path.

Tawakkul and Effort

Tawakkul is not passivity. The Prophet Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, was asked whether a person should leave his animal loose and rely on Allah, or tie it and rely on Allah. The answer in Jami‘ at-Tirmidhi 2517 was: tie it and rely. A Federal Territories Mufti article makes the same point from the wider tradition: tawakkul includes resolve, strategy, effort, supplication, and contentment with Allah’s decree.

This is exactly what Ya‘qub models. He complains to Allah, then gives his sons an instruction: go search. The heart relies on Allah, but the limbs still take the means. In modern language, tawakkul is not “I will not try.” It is “I will try without worshipping my own trying.”

What This Teaches

This ayah changed the way I think about hardship. The question is not only: will my life be better or worse if I trust Allah? The deeper question is: where will my heart go when life gets heavy?

If I trust only myself, then every closed door feels like the end of the world. If I trust only people, their limits become my ceiling. But if I trust Allah, the pain may still be there, the delay may still be there, and the uncertainty may still be there, but despair does not get the final word.

Ya‘qub did not know every detail of how the story would resolve. He knew enough about Allah to keep hope alive. Emphasizes the larger pattern of the surah: people plan, suffer, misunderstand, and wait, but Allah’s will is never defeated. That is the part I want to carry: not certainty about the timing, not control over the path, but certainty that Allah knows what I do not know.

A Practice

When something hurts, I want to practice this ayah in a simple order:

  • Name the pain honestly before Allah.
  • Ask Allah for help without trying to sound impressive.
  • Take the next halal step that is actually available.
  • Leave the hidden part of the outcome with Allah.
  • Do not let delay become despair.

Not every hardship becomes clear quickly. Some things make sense later, some in the Hereafter, and some are simply carried with adab. But Yusuf 12:86 teaches me that carrying pain to Allah is not a failure of faith. It is faith knowing where home is.
